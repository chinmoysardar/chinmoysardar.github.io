Variation of Rainwater Quality During Storage in Non-Rainy Seasons: A Case Study in a Coastal Area of Bangladesh

In salinity-affected Koyra, households depend on stored rainwater through the dry season. We followed ten household tanks for 122 days to see what happens to that water in storage.

Degree
BSc Civil Engineering (Environmental)
Department
Civil Engineering, BUET
Supervisor
Prof. Dr. Md. Abdul Jalil

M. I. U. Mazumder

Koyra, Khulna

Samples
40 · 10 tanks × 4 rounds

Study

We followed ten household tanks from the start of storage on 26 August 2024 through four sampling rounds, to 122 days. Five tanks were plastic and five were brick; five were filled from roofs and five from polythene sheets. We measured pH, turbidity, and total and fecal coliform at the BUET Environmental Engineering Laboratory, and surveyed 100 households in neighbouring villages.

Analysis: all 40 per-sample records, with repeated-measures ANOVA for change over storage time and linear mixed models, fitted with each tank as a random effect, to separate tank material from catchment surface.

My role: I carried out the field sampling, laboratory analysis and statistical analysis.

Storage timeline

  1. Day 0 · 26 Aug

    Storage begins. No coliforms detected.

  2. October

    Every tank exceeds the WHO guideline of zero.

  3. November

    Total coliform peaks in nine of the ten tanks.

  4. December · Day 122

    Plastic tanks: turbidity 0.5 NTU, coliform still 27.8 CFU/100 mL.

Key results

  • Contamination appeared in storage.No coliforms were detected at the start, but every sample taken from October onwards carried total coliform. It peaked in November at 67.2 ± 8.1 CFU/100 mL in brick tanks and 45.6 ± 5.9 in plastic tanks.
  • Tank material was associated with quality.Adjusted for sampling round and catchment, brick tanks held 2.2× more total coliform, 4.0× more fecal coliform and 3.5 NTU more turbidity than plastic tanks (p < 0.001). From October, every brick tank was worse than every plastic tank in each round. Households and tanks differed in other ways too, so this is an association, not proof of cause.
  • Catchment surface mattered much less.Roof water started 0.64 NTU more turbid than water collected on polythene sheets, but that difference closed during storage, and the coliform gap between the two was small and not significant (×1.10 for total coliform, p = 0.38).
  • Clear water was not safe water.Turbidity in plastic tanks halved about every month and reached 0.5 NTU by December, yet every sample at or below 1 NTU still carried coliforms, averaging 27.8 CFU/100 mL.
  • Households were aware of the risk.Only 35% of surveyed households considered their stored rainwater safe to drink, and 15% used no treatment. Brick-tank users reported microbial problems far more often than plastic-tank users (55% against 20%), which matches the measurements.

Tank material against catchment surface

The two design choices a household can make are not equal. Tank material changed the coliform counts several times over; the catchment surface barely moved them once the water was in storage.

Limitations and next question

This was a small study of one upazila and one storage season: ten tanks, 40 samples, four parameters and no chemical analysis. The catchment groups held only two or three tanks each, and the design is observational, so tank material cannot be separated from everything else that differs between households. It documents the problem but does not explain its mechanism. That is the question I want to pursue in graduate research.
